Introduction and Overview

This e-book is written for students in STA258 – Statistics with Applied Probability at the University of Toronto Mississauga. Its purpose is to give you a practical feel for the ideas you meet in lectures and tutorials, showing how the mathematics of probability lets us talk sensibly about uncertainty in everyday life as well as in scientific work.

Uncertainty surrounds almost everything we care about. Will tomorrow’s lecture be moved online because of a snowstorm? Can a spacecraft launch if cross-winds rise above a certain threshold? Will a new medication actually shorten recovery time once it leaves the controlled environment of a clinical trial? Each question feels different on the surface, yet the same statistical machinery helps us tackle them all. By framing an uncertain outcome as a random variable we can describe its typical behaviour, calculate the odds of unusual events and, most importantly, update those odds when new information arrives.

The human mind does a rough version of this calculation all the time. We decide to carry an umbrella because dark clouds look threatening, or we leave extra time for a commute after hearing a traffic report. But gut instinct is easily misled: streaks appear in gambling even when the dice are fair, and vivid anecdotes can drown out a mountain of quieter data. Formal probability offers a sturdier foundation. If meteorologists combine radar images, satellite readings and decades of storm records, they can say not just “it might snow,” but “there is a 70 % chance of more than ten centimetres by morning.” If engineers measure how often a particular alloy fractures under stress, they can quote the lifetime of a bridge girder with a clear margin of error rather than a hopeful guess. In each case the numbers come from a sampling distribution—an imagined catalogue of results we would see if we could repeat the experiment or observation indefinitely.

Throughout this book we will revisit that catalogue many times. We start by defining random variables and their distributions, then learn how to simulate, visualise and summarise those distributions with real data. From there we build the tools needed for inference: confidence intervals that put a reasonable bound around an unknown parameter; hypothesis tests that weigh the evidence for competing explanations; and regression models that let us tease out the influence of several factors at once. The tone is informal and example-driven—you will see as much reference to snowfall totals, pulse rates and exam scores as to abstract notation—yet every result rests on rigorous mathematics. By the end you should feel at home translating a messy, uncertain situation into symbols, pushing those symbols through the machinery of probability and translating the answer back into plain language someone with no statistical background can understand.

That ability—to move comfortably between raw experience and quantitative insight—is what STA258 is all about, and what this e-book aims to nurture.
